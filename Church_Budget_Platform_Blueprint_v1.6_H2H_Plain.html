Church Budget
Management Platform

A single place for 22 country teams to register humanitarian projects, move them through budget approval, and see where every dollar sits — replacing 22 Excel tabs, a Dynamics 365 lookup, and a mailbox full of status chasing.

This document is a blueprint, not code. Version 1.1 applies all fifteen confirmed decisions of 27 August: the approval engine now runs in-platform from status 4 to 3 only, the church’s formal approval is tracked as an external gate through “Decision Point” and CHaS, and the Regional Manager closes 3 → 2 manually. Four remarks remain open — they are listed at the end of Section 1 and none of them blocks the build.

Personas & permission matrix

Four roles, confirmed under D-02. Three sit on a single vertical ladder — a team member maintains the work, an area manager prepares and submits it, a regional manager reviews and carries it through the external gate — and the fourth, Administrator, stands outside the ladder and never approves money.

Administrator

System steward

Configures users, roles, thresholds, alert rules, master data and integrations. Sees all 22 countries. Cannot approve budget — separation of duty.

M1 · Regional Manager

Reviewer & gatekeeper

Runs Process 3 — Request approved or Return to Review — then tracks the request through Decision Point and CHaS and manually marks 3 → 2 when both confirm. Scope: assigned countries only.

M2 · Area Manager

Preparer

Owns Process 4: completes the request and raises it with Request submitted, moving the project to status 3. The stage clock on M1’s desk starts at that click.

M3 · Team Member

Project officer

Registers projects, maintains the timeline, posts notes. Drafts feed M2’s submission. Sees only their own country. The heaviest daily user.

Viewer profiles — promoted from RM-3, now v1. A built-in read-only profile mapped to a normal login. On the user record the Administrator assigns a view scope — whole area, selected countries, or named projects — and the account sees the register, dashboards and timelines inside that scope with every action hidden: no posting, no editing, no approval controls, no gate. Built for HQ observers, auditors and church leadership who need sight without touch.
Where the fourth step went. v1.0 assumed an M4 HQ tier. D-01 and D-02 replace it: the fourth step is not a person in this platform but the church’s own systems — “Decision Point” and CHaS — tracked here as an external gate that M1 updates manually.

Clock model — D-11

Every status change writes a timestamped status_event, and the platform derives days in current stage from the latest one — a counter restarts at each transition and waits for the next. Inside stage 3, the two external systems carry sub-counters of their own: days since submitted to Decision Point, days since submitted to CHaS, each stopping at its approved click.

Days in status 4Project created / returnedRequest submittedRegister, dashboard
Days in status 3 — reviewRequest submittedM1 Request approved / return / rejectInbox sort, aging widget
Gate · Decision PointDP submitted ✓DP approved ✓Gate tracker, inbox
Gate · CHaSCHaS submitted ✓CHaS approved ✓Gate tracker, inbox

Why this design survives the real world. The slowest span in the sample data — 197 days — sits between the platform’s own approval and the church’s formal one. v1.0 could only call that “waiting on HQ”; v1.1 names the system it is waiting in and counts the days against it. When the delay alert fires, it now says CHaS approval outstanding for 196 days — which is a sentence someone can act on.

Derived, never stored

Computed at read time so they can never drift from the underlying facts — the failure mode the current spreadsheet suffers from, where a stale formula produced the (46,051) day count in the sample.

  • Days in current stage — now − latest status_event.at, one counter per project, restarting at each transition (D-11)
  • Gate waiting days — per external system, (approved_at ?? now) − submitted_at
  • Total elapsed — (approved_at ?? now) − first_submitted_at, for the turnaround report
  • Budget coverage % — Σ project amounts ÷ country ceiling
  • Budget minus total — ceiling − Σ project amounts; negative renders in rose
  • Spend by status — the four-way split and each one’s share of ceiling
  • Schedule variance — today − target_end_date for anything not yet at status 1

Country scope — a rule for every dashboard, not a P2 feature. The selector accepts one country, several, or all; every widget on the dashboard recomputes from the selection — KPIs sum selected ceilings, the budget bar rescales its ceiling mark, attention rows filter, tables filter and re-sort. Four constraints: scope is global per dashboard tab (widgets cannot hold a private scope); it is remembered per dashboard between visits; it can never go empty (deselecting the last country re-selects it); and it only offers countries inside the signed-in user’s data scope, including viewer profiles. The demo implements this as one render() pass over a country array — the exact pattern each widget uses against the real API.

Conversation design

The requirement asked for a log, ticketing, notes, and a pinned direction — and for the pattern with the best track record. What follows is a short review of how the major platforms handle this, what users praise, what they complain about, and the specific model proposed here.

What the platforms do

PlatformModelPraised forCriticised for
monday.com
Updates		 A social-feed style Updates section attached to each item, with @mentions notifying individuals or teams, rich text, files and reactions. Communication happens on the work item rather than beside it; teams report replacing status emails with in-board discussion. Practitioners advise pinning key updates for visibility. Updates stay locked to their original item and do not mirror across linked boards, creating silos. Users also misread the update-count bubble as an unread count.
ClickUp
Activity tab		 A single chronological running log per task where comments, system activity and email threads interleave. Threaded replies, quote-reply, thread followers. Assigned comments — a comment can be assigned to a person and carries a Resolve checkbox; the resolver's name is shown. Unresolved assignments surface as an icon in every view and in a dedicated page. Density. A busy task's log mixes machine noise with human decisions and the important entry gets buried.
Decision-log practice
method, not product		 A structured record of what was chosen, why, who was involved, alternatives considered, expected outcome — kept concise and factual. Future readers get the reasoning without re-reading long threads; doubles as an audit trail in regulated settings. Maintained by discipline, not by the tool. Dies the moment it becomes a separate document.

Sources: monday.com support & community documentation; ClickUp help centre (Intro to comments, Assign comments, Task activity and comments); monday.com and Plane decision-log guidance; Rocketlane on dashboards that show activity instead of decision signals.

The proposed model

Take ClickUp's single chronological stream — best reviewed for preserving context across a handoff — add its assign-and-resolve mechanic, which is the most consistently praised collaboration feature in either product, and layer on the decision-log discipline as a first-class entry type rather than a separate document. Avoid monday's silo problem by making the stream the only place project conversation lives, and avoid ClickUp's density problem with filter tabs and a pinned header.

Four entry types, one stream

TypeBehaviourWhy it existsEmail?
Note Plain entry. Editable by the author with a visible edited by / at stamp. Never deletable. The everyday record — what the Notes column in the spreadsheet holds today. Only on @mention
Question Must name an assignee. Stays open with a rose marker until someone marks it resolved; resolver's name and timestamp are recorded. This is the ticketing behaviour, without a separate ticketing module. An open question is a visible obligation. Yes — on raise, on reply, on resolve
Decision Can be pinned to the project header. Only one pin at a time; pinning a new one archives the old with its date. The "conclusion / direction pin" from the brief. Answers where did we land without reading 23 entries. Yes — to owner, backup, approvers
System Machine-written, immutable, visually recessive. Status changes, approvals, alerts sent, owner changes, budget edits. Merges the audit trail into the same timeline so cause and comment sit next to each other. Never directly

Rules that keep the log usable

  • Filter tabs, not separate pages. Activity (everything) · Notes only (human entries) · Open questions · Files. Default is Activity; a user who finds it noisy switches once and the choice persists.
  • Nothing is deleted. Edits are allowed and stamped, per D-12. A budget approval record whose history can be quietly rewritten is worth less than the spreadsheet it replaced.
  • Email is generated from the stream, never parallel to it. Every alert writes a System entry saying who it went to. This is what stops email drifting back into being the real channel.
  • Replies are one level deep. Threading past that turns a project log into a forum.
  • Scroll, don't paginate. The brief asked for scrolling history; newest at top, older loading as you scroll, with a jump-to-date control for long-running projects.
  • @mention is the only notification trigger for Notes. Silence by default; deliberate noise on request.
What this replaces. Today a decision lives in one person's inbox and a status lives in a spreadsheet cell, and neither can be found six months later. After this change, the answer to "why did we hold this project" is at the top of the project page as a pinned decision, and the answer to "who is sitting on it" is a resolvable open question with a name on it.

Application type system — confirmed 28 Aug

The dashboard redesign locked a second, stricter system for the product itself, separate from this document’s type:

  • One family in-app: Source Sans 3 for everything. Hierarchy comes from weight (400 / 600 / 700) and size only. No serif, no mono on application screens — Spectral and IBM Plex Mono remain document faces.
  • Numbers are the same family with tabular figures (font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums) — columns align without a typeface switch.
  • Labels are sentence case, 12–12.5px, weight 600, slate — never tracked uppercase mono. Alert state is carried by a 3px left rule and a red value only; no tinted card backgrounds.
  • Aligned micro-layouts are componentised: the coverage cell (fixed bar + fixed value columns), the attention pill (fixed 58–60px capsule), and the legend row (fixed amount + percent columns) — so alignment survives any data width.
  • Mocks in this document for P3–P10 predate this pass — they remain layout-authoritative; the app type system above wins on styling at build time.

Type audit — 28 Aug

Audited against community (non-paid) legibility feedback rather than vendor marketing. Three consistent findings, three changes made, one platform setting added:

  • Finding — comfortable long-reading sans. Practitioner round-ups and community posts converge on Inter, Source Sans 3 and IBM Plex Sans as the safe screen defaults, with Source Sans 3 singled out as the middle ground that stays comfortable over longer reading. Change: body face moves from Public Sans to Source Sans 3 at 15.5px / 1.66, measure tightened to 76ch.
  • Finding — heavy bold and thin weights fatigue. Long-focus reading feedback favours a narrow weight range: 400 body, 600 emphasis, nothing lighter than 400 at text sizes. Change: 300-weight cuts dropped from the font load; all bold mapped to 600; display stays Spectral 400/600.
  • Finding — grey text is where documents lose readers. Secondary text at 12px needs ≥ 4.5:1 contrast. Change: muted grey darkened #7C8892 → #6F7B85 and faint #9AA4AC → #8F99A1; tabular numerals enabled on every money figure and day count so columns stop shimmering.
  • Platform setting (v1): a per-user Comfort font toggle switching the app body to Atkinson Hyperlegible — the Braille Institute face, SIL-licensed, repeatedly recommended in accessibility communities and university style guides for low-vision and long-session reading. One CSS variable; no layout change.

Competitive reference — ClickUp

ClickUp is the closest general-purpose platform to what this system does, and the one the client is most likely to be compared against. This section splits its feature set three ways: what we adopt as v1 basics, what goes on the roadmap for parity, and what we deliberately do not copy — with the reasoning for each.

Where this platform already wins

ClickUp models approvals by convention — custom statuses, an approver custom field, watchers, and a filtered “Approvals Needed” view that teams assemble themselves. Nothing enforces the chain, nothing understands a budget ceiling, and nothing represents an approval that happens in another system. Our four advantages are structural, not cosmetic:

The Regional Director lens — running 22 countries by exception

Researched separately from the ClickUp pass: what does the person above M1 — a director whose span is the whole area — actually need? The portfolio-management literature is blunt about it. A dashboard is a signal layer, not a status report: its job is to compress operational complexity into a small set of signals a decision-maker can read quickly, and the productive pattern is to circulate that live view before the review so the meeting itself is spent on decisions — accelerate, de-scope, cancel — not narration. Visibility is more than reporting: the failure mode is information that arrives too late, at the wrong altitude, or stripped of the context a decision needs. Mature portfolio tools converge on a short list — roll-ups of risks, actions, issues and decisions; cross-project timelines; capacity; budget against ceiling; exception flags. And NGO regional-director job descriptions add two duties software can carry: administrative approvals across country offices, and supervision of the people running them.

Deliberately not copied

The most consistent criticism of ClickUp in 2026 reviews is that its flexibility is its cost: teams describe it as overwhelming, and structured onboarding of two to four weeks to settle naming, status schemas and automation rules before it is productive. This platform takes the opposite bet for the opposite user — 22 country teams who open it a few times a week and must never need training:

How to read this in front of the client. The visibility and collaboration rows are mostly green for all three platforms. Those are table stakes, and v1 has them. The split happens in governance, budget and clocks: there, the two platforms offer conventions that well-behaved people can follow, while this platform offers rules that hold whether people are well-behaved or busy. The two “not offered” rows on both competitors, the external gate and its stopwatches, are the church’s actual approval process. That is the reason this is a build and not a subscription, and the roadmap items remain honestly marked and selectable in Section 11.
